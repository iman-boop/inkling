Brief · read first

Inkling — a photo of your handwriting becomes a real typeface

Assumptions I made where the brief was open: iOS phone app (capture guidance and the camera are central to the flow, so a phone is the honest frame — say the word and I'll do the desktop companion). Two primary users: someone digitising a family archive of recipes and letters, and a brand turning a founder's hand into a signature face — so the copy is warm but the artifacts are treated as valuable originals, not toys.

Design context: the Organic system — cream ground, terracotta and sage accents, Caprasimo over Figtree, 16–28px radii, pill controls, washed photography. The one thing not from the system is the handwriting itself: user artifacts are rendered in a script face as a stand-in for the real extracted glyphs, so the mockups read as content rather than brand type.

The signature moments you picked drive two screens: letters lifting off the photo into a glyph bed (review), and typing a sentence in your own hand (preview). Four screens per direction: Capture → Review → Type-to-test → Library & export.

8Motion spec — the two moments that carry the whole product

Both frames below are live and looping. One principle behind them: motion is the explanation. The lift (8a) is the only thing that tells someone their letters were taken from their page rather than matched to a stock font — so it has to be legibly the same ink moving. The write-on (8b) is the payoff, and it earns its length by mimicking a pen's pace, not a UI transition's. Everything else in the app moves in 140–220ms and gets out of the way.

Beat sheet
0–260msScan sheen sweeps the photo once, left to right. Signals reading, covers the model's real latency.
260–600Pluck. Each glyph rises 13px and scales to 1.14 in 340ms, cubic-bezier(.32,.9,.24,1). Stagger 60ms, capped at 12 visible — the rest are implied by the counter.
340–900Travel & settle. The glyph flies to its cell on an arc, landing with a 1.05 overshoot on a springy curve. The hole it left stays visibly empty on the page.
900–1180Cells resolve. Lime outline for confident, violet for uncertain — arriving after the letter lands so judgement never precedes the object.
HapticsOne soft impact on the first landing only. Thirty-eight taps is a vibrating brick.
SkippableA tap anywhere jumps to the settled state. Never gate the CTA behind the animation — it plays once per page, not per revisit.
The letter that leaves the page leaves a gap behind it — that absence is what proves the extraction is real, so never cross-fade the photo back to whole.

Rules for the write-on
Per glyph180ms fade-up from 4px with a 1.2px ink-blur burning off — the wet-to-dry read. Never a typewriter cut.
Cadence55ms between letters, +30ms after a space, +90ms after a comma or full stop. Even cadence is the tell of a machine; a hand hesitates at joins.
Real typingWhen the user types, the glyph appears on keydown with the same 180ms fade — no queue, no lag. The auto-demo only runs on the untouched field and dies at the first keystroke.
VariantsRepeat letters cycle through the alternates captured from the page. The chip calls it out once — the point of the whole product is that the second a isn't the first.
CaretSystem 1.06s blink, violet, sits on the baseline of the last glyph. It's the one element allowed to look like software.
Everything else

Standard transitions: 160ms ease-out for appearance, 220ms for screen pushes, 120ms for a control's own state. Nothing in the app animates for longer than the two moments above — they're the only places where duration is the message.

Confidence colour always cross-fades over 200ms rather than snapping, because it changes as you edit and a snap reads as an error.

Reduced motion: the lift becomes a 200ms cross-fade with the count ticking up; the write-on prints the sentence whole. Both keep the information — letters came from your page, this is your hand — and drop only the travel. The frames above honour the setting live.

Pen-paced, not UI-paced: the sentence takes ~1.3s to write, which is slow for an interface and fast for a hand.

7When the photo doesn't work — the states that keep people from blaming themselves

Two rules across all of these. Catch it in the camera, not after — a warning before the shutter costs a second, a failure after the read costs the whole attempt, so 7a intervenes live and never blocks the button (people photographing a fragile letter under bad light may have no better option). And every failure names the cause and offers the next move: no dead ends, no "something went wrong". The artifact is never the thing at fault — the light, the angle, the pen are.

Where each state sits in the flow

Before the shutter — glare, blur, page not fully in frame, too dark. Advisory chips only; the shutter never locks. Clearing the problem dismisses the chip on its own, no confirmation.

Straight after the read — no handwriting, printed type, unsupported script. Full-screen, because the run has stopped and we owe an explanation. Each one keeps the photo so nothing is lost, and each one has a route that doesn't require a new photo where physically possible.

At the review gate — partial read (7c). Not a failure: the font can be made, so the screen is a decision, not an apology. The partial badge follows the font into the library and the export sheet so it can never be quietly sold as complete.

Not shown, same pattern: export failure (retry keeps the paid entitlement), a page whose hand doesn't match the ones already in the project (offer a separate font), and a re-snap that came back worse than the first attempt (keep the better read).

Nine letters is a decision screen, not an error — with the honest cost of continuing stated in the second line.

5Locked: Acid Bloom (4b) — review, in its three modes

You were right — these aren’t competing options, they’re three modes of one screen, so all three ship behind the switcher at the top. What differs is which one opens by default: In place when the source is a photographed artifact (a recipe card, a letter) where context matters; Sheet when the font is heading for a brand kit and the whole set has to be audited; One by one on a first font, or whenever more than 8 letters came back shaky — it’s also the only mode where letter variants get chosen. Switching is free and never loses a correction. Colour does one job throughout: lime means needs you, violet means machine confidence, warm white is always the artifact.

4The finalists, 3b and 2a, across the whole flow

Both are dark-ground schemes, so the differences show up in the details: 4a uses pink for every action with lavender as the reading colour, and the paper sits warm-white against forest. 4b is higher contrast — lime carries actions and the violet second only appears on counts and sliders, so the photograph is the only soft thing on screen. Note where each one puts the “41 of 52” count and the uncertain-letter cells: pink-on-forest reads calmer, lime-on-black reads like a machine reporting.
